Analytical notes, supporting results, and response material for the eLife revision.

Goal: compare a covariance-dominated rule with the rate-dominated rule in a setting with multiple competing inhibitory-to-excitatory connections.

Three neuron motif dynamics

We consider a simple three-neuron motif, with one excitatory neuron and two inhibitory neurons, modeled as Hawkes processes (Figure 1). The excitatory neuron receives a constant input \(h_e>0\), and projects to one of the inhibitory neurons with a strong weight \(W_e\). In this setting inhibitory neuron 1 will have a strong correlation with the excitatory neuron, while inhibitory neuron 2 will be assumed as uncorrelated. The two inhibitory neurons project back to the excitatory neuron with plastic weights \(W_1\) and \(W_2\). All neurons have an exponential synaptic kernel with timescale \(\tau_{\text{syn}}.\) All synaptic weights are assumed positive. An adaptive input ensures that all inhibitory rates are fixed at \(r_i\). The excitatory rate can vary over time and is given by: \[ r_e = h_e - W_1 \,r_i - W_2 \,r_i = h_e - (W_1 + W_2) \,r_i \tag{1}\]

The two inhibitory-to-excitatory synapses \(W_1\) and \(W_2\) are plastic, following identical symmetric plasticity rules. The average synaptic weight dynamics are given by: \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{d\,W_1}{dt} &= \alpha_{\text{pre}} \,r_i + \alpha_{\text{post}} \,r_e + B \,r_i \,r_e - M^{10}\,W_1\,r_i + M^{01}\, W_e \, r_e \\ \frac{d\,W_2}{dt} &= \alpha_{\text{pre}} \,r_i + \alpha_{\text{post}} \,r_e + B \,r_i \,r_e - M^{10}\,W_2\,r_i \end{aligned} \tag{2}\] Where \(B\) is the area under the STDP curve, and the \(M\) terms represent the first-order motif contributions to plasticity (ignoring higher order contributions). Since here we only consider symmetric rules \(M^{10} = M^{01} := M\).

In the Vogels-Spreckeler rule we have: \[ \alpha_{\text{post}}^{\text{Vog}} = 0, \quad B^{\text{Vog}} = 1 \tag{3}\] The \(M^{\text{Vog}}\) term is: \[ M^{\text{Vog}} = \frac{1}{2\,\left(\tau_+ + \tau_{\text{syn}} \right)} \tag{4}\] And \(\alpha_{\text{pre}}^{\text{Vog}}\) is a free parameter set to ensure that the rate of the excitatory neuron is maintained at a target value \(r_e^{\text{(target)}}\).

In the covariance-dominated rule instead: \[ \quad B^{\text{cov}} = 0 ,\quad M^{\text{cov}} = \frac{1}{2\,\left(\tau_+ + \tau_{\text{syn}} \right)} - \frac{1}{2\,\left(\tau_- + \tau_{\text{syn}} \right)} \tag{5}\] And both \(\alpha_{\text{pre}}^{\text{cov}}\) and \(\alpha_{\text{post}}^{\text{cov}}\) are free parameters that can be used to set the target rate. Here for simplicity we will set \(\alpha_{\text{post}}^{\text{cov}} = 0\), but all our results hold in the general case (see detailed analytic notes).

For \(\tau_- \gg \tau_+\) and \(\tau_- \gg \tau_{\text{syn}}\), \(M^{\text{Vog}}\) and \(M^{\text{cov}}\) are similar. The main difference between the rules is therefore in the \(B^{\text{Vog}}=1\) and \(B^{\text{cov}}=0\) terms.

Fixed point solution

General

If the stable fixed-point conditions are fulfilled (see detailed analytic notes), the excitatory rate and weights converge to

\[ \begin{aligned} r_e^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)} &= \frac{M\,h_e-2\,\alpha_{\text{pre}}\,r_i} {M\,(1+W_e)+2\,(\alpha_{\text{post}}+B\,r_i)},\\ W_1^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)} &= \frac{h_e+(W_e-1)\,r_e^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)}}{2\,r_i}, \\ W_2^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)} &= \frac{h_e-(1+W_e)\,r_e^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)}}{2\,r_i}. \end{aligned} \tag{6}\]

Setting the fixed-point rate equal to \(r_e^{\text{(target)}}\) requires

\[ \alpha_{\text{pre}} = -\frac{\alpha_{\text{post}}+B\,r_i}{r_i}\,r_e^{\text{(target)}} + \frac{M}{2\,r_i}\, \left[h_e-(1+W_e)\,r_e^{\text{(target)}}\right]. \tag{7}\]

TipMost important result here!

The final weights depend on the plasticity rule parameters only through \(r_e^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)}\). Therefore, any rule that reaches a specific excitatory target rate as a stable fixed point, will also have the same stable final inhibitory weights!!! In this setting the stable solution is rule-invariant, no matter if rate-dominated or covariance dominated, and the only difference between rules is in the dynamics of convergence to the fixed point!

Vogels-Sprekeler rule

With \(\alpha_{\text{post}}^{\text{Vog}}=0\) and \(B^{\text{Vog}}=1\),

\[ r_e^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)} = \frac{M^{\text{Vog}}\,h_e-2\,\alpha_{\text{pre}}^{\text{Vog}}\,r_i} {M^{\text{Vog}}\,(1+W_e)+2\,r_i}, \qquad \alpha_{\text{pre}}^{\text{Vog}} = -r_e^{\text{(target)}} + \frac{M^{\text{Vog}}}{2\,r_i}\, \left[h_e-(1+W_e)\,r_e^{\text{(target)}}\right]. \tag{8}\]

Covariance-based rule

With \(\alpha_{\text{post}}^{\text{cov}}=0\) and \(B^{\text{cov}}=0\), assuming \(M^{\text{cov}}>0\) (thus \(\tau_->\tau_+\)),

\[ r_e^{\left( t \rightarrow \infty \right)} = \frac{h_e}{1+W_e} - \frac{2\,\alpha_{\text{pre}}^{\text{cov}}\,r_i} {M^{\text{cov}}\,(1+W_e)}, \qquad \alpha_{\text{pre}}^{\text{cov}} = \frac{M^{\text{cov}}}{2\,r_i}\, \left[h_e-(1+W_e)\,r_e^{\text{(target)}}\right]. \tag{9}\]

Convergence of sum and difference modes

Although the fixed point is rule-invariant, the convergence dynamics are not. To analyze the convergence dynamics, we define sum and difference modes of the inhibitory weights.

\[ W_s := W_1+W_2, \qquad W_d := W_1-W_2. \tag{10}\] We use \(\tau_s\) and \(\tau_d\) to denote the relaxation times of the sum and difference modes, respectively.

The excitatory rate depends only on the sum mode (see equation Equation 1), therefore the convergence of sum mode is equivalent to the convergence of the excitatory rate to \(r_e^{\text{(target)}}\). However, a full separation of weights implies that the difference mode has reached convergence.

Let \(\eta>0\) be the learning rate multiplying the right-hand side of each plasticity equation in Equation 2 (the equations above use \(\eta=1\)). This rescales the convergence times without changing the fixed point.

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_s &= \frac{1} {\eta\,r_i\,\left[M\,(1+W_e)+2\,\alpha_{\text{post}}+2\,B\,r_i\right]}, \\ \tau_d &= \frac{1}{\eta\,M\,r_i}. \end{aligned} \tag{11}\]

The ratio of the sum-mode timescale to the difference-mode timescale is

\[ \frac{\tau_s}{\tau_d} = \frac{M}{M\,(1+W_e)+2\,(\alpha_{\text{post}}+B\,r_i)}. \tag{12}\]

Since the sum drives the difference through \(r_e\), the difference generally contains both timescales.

Vogels-Sprekeler rule

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_s^{\text{Vog}} &= \frac{1} {\eta^{\text{Vog}}\,r_i\,\left[M^{\text{Vog}}\,(1+W_e)+2\,r_i\right]}, \\ \tau_d^{\text{Vog}} &= \frac{1}{\eta^{\text{Vog}}\,M^{\text{Vog}}\,r_i}.\\ \frac{\tau_s^{\text{Vog}}}{\tau_d^{\text{Vog}}} &= \frac{M^{\text{Vog}}} {M^{\text{Vog}}\,(1+W_e)+2\,r_i}. \end{aligned} \tag{13}\]

For \(M^{\text{Vog}}\ll2\,r_i\), expanding gives

\[ \frac{\tau_s^{\text{Vog}}}{\tau_d^{\text{Vog}}} = \frac{M^{\text{Vog}}}{2\,r_i} - \frac{(1+W_e)\,(M^{\text{Vog}})^2}{4\,r_i^2} + \mathcal{O}\!\left((M^{\text{Vog}})^3\right). \tag{14}\]

Therefore \(\tau_s \ll \tau_d\) , the sum relaxes much faster than the difference in this regime.

Covariance-based rule

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_s^{\text{cov}} &= \frac{1} {\eta^{\text{cov}}\,M^{\text{cov}}\,r_i\,(1+W_e)}, \\ \tau_d^{\text{cov}} &= \frac{1}{\eta^{\text{cov}}\,M^{\text{cov}}\,r_i}. \end{aligned} \tag{15}\]

\[ \frac{\tau_s^{\text{cov}}}{\tau_d^{\text{cov}}} = \frac{1}{1+W_e}. \tag{16}\]

This ratio is independent of \(M^{\text{cov}}\) and in our parameter range \(\mathcal{O}(1)\).

Matching the sum-mode timescales

For the same \(r_i\) and \(W_e\), imposing \(\tau_s^{\text{Vog}}=\tau_s^{\text{cov}}\) requires

\[ \frac{\eta^{\text{Vog}}}{\eta^{\text{cov}}} = \frac{M^{\text{cov}}\,(1+W_e)} {M^{\text{Vog}}\,(1+W_e)+2\,r_i}. \tag{17}\]

Conclusions

Although the fixed point is rule-invariant, in the Vogels-Sprekeler rule the sum mode relaxes much faster than the difference mode, while in the covariance-dominated rule the two modes relax on similar timescales. Therefore, the covariance-dominated rule is more effective at separating inhibitory weights, and existing work based on Vogels-Sprekeler might simply ignore or underestimate this very slow adaptation, “masking” is under other effects and variations.
